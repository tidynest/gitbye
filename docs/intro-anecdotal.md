# GitBye

It was a Tuesday. I know it was a Tuesday because I went back and checked,
later, once I had become the sort of person who goes back and checks.

`@velocity_dan` followed me at 03:14. I was asleep. In the morning there was the
little notification and I thought: nice. Somebody found the parser. You get
maybe four of those a year and they are quietly lovely, so I clicked follow back
the way you nod at someone who holds a door.

His bio said "Building in public. Shipping daily. 10x mindset." His pinned
repository was a fork of a Rust tutorial, last commit 2021, README still saying
`your-project-name-here`. I noticed that, thought it was a bit sad, and did not
think about it again for nine days.

On the ninth day he was gone.

Not dramatically. There is no notification for being unfollowed, which is the
whole reason this works. I only found out because I happened to be scrolling my
followers looking for someone else entirely, and the name that should have been
between `@vasquez` and `@vex` was not there. I sat looking at that gap for
longer than it deserved.

Then I did the thing you are not supposed to do, which is go and look at his
profile.

Followers: 4,203. Following: 11.

Eleven. I counted them, because at that point I had lost all dignity. His mum
(same surname, no repositories, one starred gist from 2019). Linus Torvalds.
Three accounts that post exclusively about AI agents. A recruiter. And five
people I could not identify, one of whom I am fairly sure owes him money.

Four thousand people had done what I did, and eleven of us had been kept.

Here is the bit that actually got me. It was not the unfollow. People are
allowed to change their minds, get bored, tidy up, decide the parser is not for
them. It was the arithmetic. To make that number on his profile, he had to do
this to four thousand individual human beings, one at a time, on a schedule, and
be counting on every single one of them not noticing. It was not carelessness.
Noticing was the failure case, and he had accounted for it.

So I started paying attention, which is a mistake I recommend to nobody.

`@ship_it_kai` lasted six days. `@buildinpublic_max` lasted eleven, and had the
audacity to star a repository on his way past, presumably because a star costs
nothing and makes the follow look considered. Over about four months I watched
roughly forty accounts arrive, collect, and leave, and every one of them had the
same profile: enormous follower count, tiny following count, contribution graph
showing one green fortnight in 2022 and then tundra, and somewhere a repository
called `awesome-something` with 400 stars, which is a bulleted list of links to
other people's work, curated in the sense that somebody once pressed enter.

`@velocity_dan` came back in March. Followed me again. I assume the list had
cycled round.

I did not follow back, and I felt petty about it for a day and a half, and then
I felt fine, and then I wrote this.

That is all GitBye is. It writes down who followed whom first, and when. If
somebody follows you, you return it like a normal person, and they leave shortly
afterwards, it takes your follow back so their number stops being partly made of
you. It does nothing to people who followed you and stayed. It does nothing to
people you chose to follow first, ever, because that was your decision and it is
none of the software's business.

It just means that the next time somebody works out that being liked is a
numbers game, they will have to play it with somebody else's name in the column.
